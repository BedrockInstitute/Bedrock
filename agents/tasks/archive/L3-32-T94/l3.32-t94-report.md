## 0. Verdict

**The ideal top-level decomposition of the rud route is 18 modules, and it
is the standing 22 minus four dead modules, with StepInL in its ideal form.**
The standing module boundaries are structurally sound: the 22 modules form a
clean layered dependency DAG whose cut already follows the deliverables
(value layer, tower layer, definability layer, bridge and landing layer,
order layer). The architecture problem is not in the boundaries. It is in
three places, each with a different answer:

1. **Four modules exist for a route that has since changed, and they are
   retirement candidates, not rewrite candidates.** BaseBlock, CodePred and
   CodeSet are already in the D18 retirement set (dev/ledger.toml:73-91),
   and their consumers died with the satisfaction-internalization cone.
   OpGraph is this report's finding: it was built to fill W3's
   variable-form op-graph gap, and T63 refuted that gap by finding StepInL
   already carries all sixteen graphs at arbitrary arguments
   (src/L/Rud/StepInL.lagda.md:2139-2160). OpGraph covers five of the
   sixteen operations, in the outer world, and nobody imports it except
   `Everything`.
2. **StepInL is one over-sized mixed-content module whose ideal form is one
   module, priced and scoped already.** The `stepinl-rewrite` row
   (dev/ledger.toml:136-143), re-scoped by T78, prices the ideal-form
   replacement at 1.24 to 1.73k naive / 1.61 to 2.25k calibrated, all x1.3,
   and the standing chapter archives wholesale under D17. The replacement
   carries the sixteen-operation variable-form content W3 and the bridge
   need; the pair/junk reads that only CodePred consumed die with the
   cluster.
3. **The seconds live in the modules that state at concrete tower positions,
   and there the discipline is P-k, R-35 and R-38, not carrier
   abstraction.** Bridge (185 to 199 s after the P-k fix, depending on the
   profile), Images (51 s, three unsealed `right-spec` sites), HF and Finite
   (20 s and 10 s on the dated profile, deliberate first-limit instances),
   CodeSet (18 s, retires), StepInL (54 to 84 s, rewritten). The modules
   that can be stated at an abstract carrier already are (Hierarchy,
   SatSets, Describe, Order, LevelSigma, Ops, Switch), and they are all at
   or below the benchmark on the dated profile (section 5).

**The price, both calibers, from the rewrite side (D17; D-19 not quotable
here):** the route today is 2,884 signatures over 12,147 lines at 458 s,
0.159 s/sig (the F0-corrected count; dev/PLAN.md:1159). Retirement of the
four dead modules removes 440 signatures, 2,057 lines and about 40 s on the
dated profile. The StepInL replacement adds 1.24 to 1.73k naive lines at
x1.3. After those ruled moves the route sits at about 0.16 s/sig, still
more than double the 0.074 exit. Closing the gap requires Bridge to reach
the benchmark (about 185 s of the 246 s prize, dev/PLAN.md:1160) plus the small
concrete-module fixes (Images R-38, HF/Finite R-35, each tens of seconds).
**The exit condition is therefore a statement on the B1 gate: if Bridge's
rewrite plateaus where its sealing plateaued (0.97 to 1.04 s/sig on the
corrected count, thirteen to fourteen times the benchmark), the route cannot reach 0.074, and the
honest exit is the measured floor, not the benchmark.** That is the real
content of section 5.

**The stop the brief allows is not taken, but its near-form is.** The honest
finding is not "the decomposition is ideal, keep the per-module seconds
programme". It is: the decomposition is ideal after four retirements and the
StepInL rewrite, and the remaining seconds programme is a statement
discipline programme inside the concrete-position modules. The seconds-
sorted batch plan the owner rejected was wrong in two ways this recon
documents: it funded in check-time order rather than consumer order, and it
booked CodeSet, a D18-retiring module, as a rewrite batch (section 5.3).

**Why the cut falls where it does.** Three criteria decide each boundary:
(1) a distinct deliverable face: values and specs (Ops, Images), the tower
(Hierarchy, Step, OrdBlocks), the definability walks (Describe, Switch,
SatSets, LevelSigma, StepInL), the bridge and its landing (Bridge, DefInJ,
SatTable, Finite, HF), the order (Order, ClassJ); (2) a distinct consumer
set, so a module's removal or change has a named blast radius; (3) a uniform
statement discipline, so each module is checked by the rule its content
permits. The dependency DAG is acyclic (verified in section 6), and every
edge points from a cheaper, more abstract module toward the concrete bridge
and landing modules, which is the direction the check-cost evidence says is
cheap (section 7).

Two boundaries were real decisions, stated plainly:

- **StepInL is one module, not two.** The T7 per-part table (cited in T78
  section 3) splits the chapter into the reads (die with CodePred), the
  values (bridge), the memberships and graphs (W3 and bridge), and the step
  formula (W3 and bridge). The last three share one frame: the sixteen
  memberships are the graphs' substrate (`graphOf i = eqFrame f2 (memOf i)`,
  StepInL:2139-2140), and the step formula is the graphs' disjunction
  (`bigOr` at StepInL:2182-2184). Splitting them would duplicate the frame;
  the priced ideal form is one module.
- **SatTable stays its own module.** It is 109 lines and 21 signatures, its
  content is the landing's successor-step shape, and folding it into Bridge
  would move concrete-position seconds into the method-gate module without
  removing them.

## 3. The duplication finding: patterns, instances, and the generic cost

The D29 question, answered with the measurements that exist. The route's
size is dominated by sixteen-operation content: Ops (551 lines), Images
(341), Describe (1036), Step (755), StepInL (1989), SatSets (1289), CodePred
(1186) and OpGraph (285) sum to 7,432 of the 12,147 route lines (61
percent). The question is how much of that is one pattern written sixteen
times, and the honest measured answer is: **the per-op frames are already
generic, each operation's membership content is genuinely distinct
mathematics, and the removable pattern share is a few hundred lines, not
thousands.** The one large pattern that existed, the two-tower mirror, is
already removed.

### 3.1 The two-tower mirror: the largest pattern, already removed

`L.LevelFormula` and `L.Rud.LevelSigma` stated the same clause kit at two
towers: 265 byte-identical lines, 388 of LevelSigma's 788 lines at 0.85

asks about, delivered once with its numbers. Its lesson for the route: a
clause kit shared by two carriers is the highest-value generic move, and the
route's remaining per-op worlds do not share carriers in the same way.

### 3.3 Why the sixteen operations are not sixteen instances of one pattern

The D29 question has a trap, and the route is its demonstration. The sixteen
operations of the Schindler-Zeman basis are not sixteen instances of one
mathematical fact; they are sixteen operations with sixteen different
extension specifications (unordered pair, difference, product, middle
insertion, right append, union, domain, membership relation, collection,
ordered pair, single image, slice collection, four pair-value operations,
relativization slot). Each op world (values, constant-form descriptions,
variable-form memberships, satisfaction sets) must state each operation's
distinct content once. What repeated is the *shell around the content* in
each world, and the route has already factored those shells to the point
where the measured residue is a few hundred lines (section 3.2). The
obligation-level reading agrees: the 2,884 signatures are dominated by
distinct membership proofs (StepInL's sixteen adequacy arms, SatSets' atom
and engine clauses, Describe's sixteen descriptions), not by re-stated
clauses. The single largest obligation-level pattern, the two-tower clause
kit, is gone.

### 3.5 What a generic form would cost, stated in both shapes
