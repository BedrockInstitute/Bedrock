### 1.5 What that means, and why it changes the fix

The failure is **not** that agents were given too much and read too little.
The agents read what they were pointed at. The failure is one layer up:

> **The orchestrator selects which rules to cite from MEMORY, and memory is a
> cache that was never invalidated. Sixteen Rules and a twenty-case playbook
> landed on 2026-08-01 and 08-02, and the citation habit had already formed
> around the earlier era's entries. Nothing in the process noticed.**

The owner's layered import helps, but the load-bearing part is not "make the
core small". It is **"make the selection mechanical rather than recalled"**,
and above all **"make a newly added rule impossible to ignore"**.

## 2. The proposed solution

### Layer 0: the core, always loaded, hard-capped

One file, `dev/CORE.md`, admitted to by a strict test: a rule belongs here only
if it applies to **every** task regardless of kind **and** has a measured cost
behind it. **Hard cap 1,200 words.** The cap is the design: a core that grows
is a core that stops being read, and a rule that cannot displace an existing
one does not belong.

Candidates, on today's evidence: D-10 (check the target's truth first), C-22
(write the deliverable incrementally), D-1 (gate before funding), the
both-dimensions rule, the transplant law from P-l, and the evidence-is-`file:line`
rule.

### Layer 1: task-kind bundles, selected by a script rather than by memory

`dev/rules.toml` declares, for each task kind, the rule IDs that are mandatory:

```toml
[bundle.build]
ids = ["P-h", "P-k", "P-l", "R-35", "R-36", "R-38", "I-4", "I-5", "C-12", "Rule 1", "Rule 2", "Rule 14", "P-i"]
[bundle.probe]
ids = ["D-1", "D-22", "C-12", "P-i"]
[bundle.rewrite]
ids = ["D-17", "D-19-is-not-quotable", "P-h", "P-l", "Rule 5", "Rule 13"]
```

`scripts/rules.py --for build` prints the IDs with their one-line statements.
**The orchestrator pastes that output; it does not recall a set.**

### Layer 2: everything else, reachable by trigger

`dev/rules.toml` also carries trigger terms per rule (`opaque`, `subst`,
`heap`, `with`, `PT.rec`, ...). `scripts/rules.py --grep <term>` returns the
matching IDs. This is the owner's keyword layer, and it is where the long tail
lives without costing context.

### The enforcement point, which is what makes it a rule and not a wish

Two mechanical refusals, in the same "refuse, do not warn" style that fixed the
dispatch notifier today:

1. **`dispatch.py check <brief>` REFUSES a brief that does not carry its
   declared kind's layer-1 bundle.** The brief declares `kind:` in its header;
   the checker compares the cited IDs against `rules.toml` and fails on a
   missing mandatory ID.
2. **`scripts/check-rules.py` REFUSES a `dev/LESSONS.md` entry that belongs to
   no bundle and has no trigger term.** This is the fix for the actual failure:
   on 2026-08-01 sixteen Rules entered the corpus and entered no bundle, so
   nothing ever cited them. Under this check, that commit would not have
   passed the gate.

**Clause 2 is the one that matters.** Clause 1 stops a careless brief; clause 2
stops the corpus from silently growing a region nobody visits.

### What this does NOT do, stated so review can attack it

It does not make agents read better, it does not reduce the corpus, and it does
not help when a rule is cited and misapplied, which is what happened with
`[T88]`'s confounded experiment and with the R-38 anchor that measured zero.
Those are transplant failures, and P-l covers them. This proposal addresses
exactly one failure mode: **a rule that exists and is never brought to the
work.**

## 3. Cost

`dev/CORE.md` about 120 lines; `dev/rules.toml` one row per rule, 128 rows;
`scripts/rules.py` about 120 lines; the two checker hooks about 60 lines.
Roughly a day's dispatch, and the recurring cost is one `rules.py` call per
brief.

## 5. The refutation, and what survived it

`[L3.32-T105]` verified every figure in section 1 and found them right: 112
briefs, 10 citing the playbook, nine of those today, and the corpus and date
tables all reproduce. **Then it killed the inference, on three independent
grounds.**

**(a) The preventing rules did not exist.** For two of the three expensive
modules, the law that covers the defect was written **on 2026-08-06, after the
defect was found**. `Bridge`'s defect is covered by **P-k**, added in
`f76c877` today; and P-k's own text says why nothing older reached it: R-36 and
R-38 say seal an unfolding read lemma and seal it at birth, **both were
followed**, the seal was in the right place and the STATEMENT was in the wrong
place. `SquareLaw`'s is covered by **P-l**, added in `b4a4dd7` today. **So the
gap was missing rules, not unread ones**, and no amount of citation routing
would have helped.

**(b) The playbook is applied where it was never cited.** `[T100]` swept the
trunk outside the three modules and found **one violation in 58 modules**, in
code whose briefs never named a single `Rule N`. Citation is not application,
in either direction, so a low citation count is not evidence of a low
application rate.

**(c) The briefs that built the three modules were the thinnest citers in the
corpus**, which means the story "a stale habit cited the wrong set" does not
even describe those briefs accurately.

**And the mechanism has its own defects**, the sharpest being that **my own
example bundle contained a rule ID that does not exist**
(`"D-19-is-not-quotable"`), which is precisely the drift the reviewer predicted
from splitting a rule's text in `LESSONS.md` from its routing in a second file.
It is also escapable by trigger terms or bundle bloat, its enforcement point
sat in `.claude/` rather than in the tree, and above all **it enforces
citation, which the evidence says is not application**.

### What survived, and is being implemented

1. **The new laws are the actual fix, and they now exist.** P-k and P-l go into
   the standing brief clauses so the citation habit refreshes immediately,
   without any routing layer.
2. **Validate rule IDs mechanically**, since the proposal itself shipped a
   fake one. Cheap, in the tree, and it catches a real class.
3. **The measurement gate dominates.** `scripts/check-timing.py` is the only
   thing that could have caught all three modules, and `[T105]` says so
   plainly: had it existed on 2026-08-03, it would have flagged every one.
   Extending its coverage beats any amount of context engineering.

**The layer-0/1/2 machinery is not built.**
